# Internal team ask — bulleted-questions shape

When Rachael is asking her XRAY teammates for input on something she's planning (a new offering, a change to how something is run, a request for their perspective), her voice uses this shape:

1. One-sentence context + the ask ("would you be up for sharing your thoughts, opinions, insights?").
2. Bulleted list of the actual questions.
3. "Some benefits:" paragraph.
4. "Topic ideas:" or equivalent labeled section for anything more detailed.

No sign-off (Slack). No "All best, Rachael."

## Canonical example (locked 2026-08-05)

Slack DM to Aris and Doc about launching XRAY Tech Talk (open office hours for prospects). Rachael sent this verbatim after editing Claude's earlier prose-heavy draft.

```
Hi, Aris and Doc, Tom and I were kicking around XRAY Tech Talk (open office hours where prospects can drop in and ask technical questions), and, since y'all are talking to leads, would you be up for sharing your thoughts, opinions, insights?


Do you think this would be helpful?
What day and time would work for prospects?
Hosting every other week?
What are prospects asking about most


Some benefits:
Feeding Doc material for the newsletter and, since I stalk all the frontier AI companies, wowing prospects with the most up-to-date info :joy: Most weeks I'll likely be hanging out by myself, and that's fine; that we offer open hours is another signal of credibility, even when no one shows.



Topic ideas:
The prescient question I'm hearing right now is: if anyone can build, what do consultants still offer?
1. Schema and data design (I haven't had a client who could pull this off on their own); 2. Judgment on scope, i.e. what goes into the MVP vs. saved for later (so much of my job is pruning a client's ideas until what's left is streamlined and focused);
3. Identifying and developing the user journey (I've mapped these enough that I've got an intuitive sense for spotting quick wins and weak points)
```

## The four principles this example is teaching (Rachael, 2026-08-05)

1. **Concise without being redundant.** Each sentence carries one idea. No restating the same point in a new phrasing. If a benefit is named once, it doesn't get named again in the topics section.
2. **Not pedantic.** No trailing explanatory clauses (", so I'm holding off until you weigh in"). No spelling out what the reader already understands. The ask itself implies the wait.
3. **Ask questions, don't tell.** "Do you think this would be helpful?" not "I need to know if this is helpful." "What day and time would work?" not "I want to know what day works." Every question is a real open question, not a rhetorical setup for a decision Rachael has already made.
4. **Short out of respect for their time.** Aris and Doc get one screen of text. If it fits in a single Slack panel without scrolling, they can read and respond in a minute. Long messages read as "your time matters less than my need to explain."

## What to copy from this into future drafts

1. Ask lives in the opener; benefits and topics come after.
2. Questions stack as separate lines, not woven into a paragraph. One question per line.
3. Colloquial ("y'all"), three-word combos ("thoughts, opinions, insights"), not one clinical word.
4. Section labels ("Some benefits:", "Topic ideas:") do the structural work; no headers or bold.
5. Positive framing paired with the negative ("quick wins and weak points," not just "weak points").
6. Active verbs ("identifying and developing"), not passive ("reading").
7. Keep expressive openers ("since I stalk all the frontier AI companies") — that's the voice.
8. Slack drafts skip the "All best, Rachael" close.
9. Blank lines between sections; single blank line between bullet blocks; two blank lines above section labels for visual breathing room.

## What to strip

- "I want your read" / "I'd appreciate your read before I move on it" → mechanical, formal, cut it.
- "Where I need you:" → client-email framing, wrong voice for teammate ask.
- Prose paragraphs listing questions inside sentences.
- Sign-off on chat-style comms.
- Any sentence that explains a sentence you already wrote.
- Any sentence that turns a real question into a stated preference ("I was thinking bi-weekly" is a tell; "Hosting every other week?" is the ask).
